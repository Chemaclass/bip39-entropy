Entropy · a field guide

Twelve words are
a number

Everything that keeps that number yours is decided in the instant it is generated. This page shows the arithmetic rather than asserting it, for someone who has heard the word entropy without being told what it measures.

The short version

  • Entropy counts how many numbers yours could have been. It measures how you picked it, never how random the words look.
  • The list holds 2048 words because that is two to the eleventh, so one word is exactly eleven bits.
  • The checksum catches about fifteen copying mistakes in sixteen and says nothing about how guessable your phrase is.
  • Never take randomness from your own head, because words you choose are worth a few dozen bits.
  • Every wallet emptied by weak randomness was emptied because the number was small. The standard held every time.

Warning

Never mark your own words on a printed word list. Twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is a plaintext copy of your recovery phrase, and an easier one to read than your handwriting.

Never type a real recovery phrase into any website, including this one. This page has no field that accepts one, makes no network requests and stores nothing. Anything offering to “validate” or “recover” a phrase online is a theft pattern.

01 What a seed phrase is

A BIP-39 phrase is a number written in words. Each word is an index into a fixed, public list of 2048 entries, so abandon is 0 and zoo is 2047, and nothing else about a word matters.

Your wallet doesn't store the words: it joins them, runs PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512 over the string for 2048 iterations, and derives every key from the 512-bit seed that comes out. That is why the same words rebuild the same wallet anywhere, forever.

So a phrase is worth exactly as much as its number is unpredictable. The word list, the derivation, the arithmetic and this poster are all public. Only the number was ever secret.

02 Entropy, defined properly

Entropy is not a measure of how random something looks. It counts how many equally likely possibilities a secret was drawn from, in bits, so n bits means 2^n candidates and each extra bit doubles an attacker's work. A 12-word phrase carries 128 bits, which is about 3.4 × 10^38 candidates.

One handle on a number that size. Bitcoin's mining network is the largest purpose-built computing effort that has ever existed, running on the order of 10^21 hashes per second. At that rate, merely counting to 2^128 takes about 11 billion years, roughly the present age of the universe. A bare hash is far cheaper than one full trial of a phrase, so that underestimates the real cost.

Randomness is a property of the process, not of the output. From a fair source, 0000…0000 is exactly as likely as any other string, so a phrase that “looks random” tells you nothing. Entropy is measured against what an attacker knows about your process, and that is why the next section is about people rather than mathematics.

03 Why 2048 words

2048 is 2^11, and that is the whole reason for the number. Eleven bits address exactly 2048 entries with nothing left over, so converting bits to words is a straight slice of the bit string. A list of 2000 words wouldn't divide the space evenly, and selecting from it without bias would need rejection sampling.

It is also why the poster can be a grid whose coordinates are the index. hamster is 837, which is 01101000101 in eleven bits.

Twelve words therefore carry 12 × 11 = 132 bits. BIP-39 does not spend all 132 on entropy. It generates ENT bits of randomness, appends ENT ÷ 32 checksum bits, and splits the result into eleven-bit groups. For twelve words that is 128 bits of entropy plus a 4-bit checksum; for twenty-four, 256 plus 8.

The last column is a consequence worth knowing: the final word is never free. With the first eleven fixed, only 128 of the 2048 words complete a valid 12-word phrase, so a sequence written down at random is valid about one time in sixteen.

The roller shows the same fact from your side of the table. Once your entropy is thrown, the last word has sixteen consecutive candidates on the list, and the checksum picks one. It draws the sixteen and marks yours.

Whether 12 or 24 words is better is not settled by entropy alone, because 128 bits is already beyond brute force by the margins above. 24 words is chiefly a hedge against future cryptanalysis and against Grover-style quantum search, which in theory halves the effective strength of a symmetric secret. Whether that speedup is ever practical is disputed, and longer phrases cost you more to record and to check.

04 The checksum

The checksum bits are the first ENT ÷ 32 bits of SHA-256 of the raw entropy, appended before the phrase is cut into words. That is why the last word mixes entropy and checksum rather than sitting apart.

What it catches: a word mistyped into another real word, two words in the wrong order, a word read off the wrong row. Any single such mistake survives only if it happens to produce the right four bits, about one time in sixteen, so roughly fifteen errors in sixteen are caught.

What it does not catch matters more. A phrase you assembled from twelve chosen words passes exactly as readily as one from dice. An attacker enumerates entropy values and computes the checksum as the standard prescribes, so those four bits cost them nothing. “My wallet accepted it” says nothing about how guessable it is.

You can watch these bits get made. Throw dice on the roller and it computes the checksum from your entropy, labels the four bits in amber, and shows where they land in the last word.

05 Where the randomness comes from

A person is not a random number generator, and this is not a matter of trying harder. Asked for a random digit, people over-choose 7 and shy away from 0 and 5.

Asked for a sequence they avoid repeats and long runs, and asked for words they reach for concrete nouns, names and dates. Every one of those tendencies is a pattern an attacker can rank candidates by, and an attacker who ranks does not have to enumerate.

The arithmetic is stark. Choose each of your twelve words uniformly from a shortlist of the 16 you find most memorable, which is unusual discipline. That is 4 bits per word and 48 bits for the phrase, against the 128 the format implies. Widen the shortlist to 32 words and you reach 60 bits, which a serious hardware budget still works through. The words look identical either way.

06 When entropy failed

Everything above this point is arithmetic. What follows is the record. In every case the software worked: keys were valid, transactions confirmed, and where there was a phrase the checksum passed. Nobody could have caught any of it by studying their own words. Three mechanisms are worth walking through, and the table carries the full record.

  • A careful change that removed the randomness. Debian's OpenSSL, 2006 to 2008. A maintainer removed two lines that fed uninitialised memory into the random pool, which Valgrind had correctly flagged. That left the process id as the only varying input, and Linux hands out at most 32,768 of those, so every key came from fifteen bits (CVE-2008-0166). Patching fixed nothing, because the keys already existed.
  • A secret a person chose. Brainwallets, from around 2011. A passphrase you invented went through a single unsalted SHA-256 and became the private key, so the whole mapping could be precomputed. The 2016 Financial Cryptography study found 884 funded brainwallets and all but 21 had been drained, half within 21 minutes of funding. A human-chosen passphrase is worth about 20 bits.
  • A generator seeded with 32 bits. Milk Sad, 2023. Libbitcoin Explorer's bx seed used a Mersenne Twister seeded with 32 bits of system clock, so asking for 256 bits returned one clock reading stretched to fill the space. The output passed every test a user could apply, because the shortfall was in the input. The same mistake produced Profanity and the Trust Wallet extension.

Set the collapses side by side. The middle column is what the format implies: 256 bits for a raw private key, 128 for a 12-word phrase, at least that for an SSH or TLS key. The one beside it is what was there.

The three 32-bit cases deserve the arithmetic spelled out. 32 bits doesn't sound small until you count it. 2^32 is 4294967296, about 4.3 billion. A single machine testing a million phrases a second, PBKDF2 and all, walks that entire space in a little over an hour.

The published estimates agree, case by case.

  • Trust Wallet. A couple of hours on one computer for every phrase the extension could ever have produced.
  • Profanity. Under ten hours on a laptop to precompute every key the tool could generate.
  • Milk Sad. At most a few days on a gaming machine for the whole of Libbitcoin's seed space.

That is not a hardware budget. It is an afternoon.

None of this was a failure of BIP-39. Wherever the standard was in the picture, it held. The word list was the same public 2048 words, correctly indexed. The derivation was correct. The checksum was correct, and it passed every time, because a well-formed phrase is the only thing it was ever asked to certify.

What failed was the number underneath, and the number is the one part of the system nobody can inspect afterwards. A phrase does not record its own origin. There is no field in it for provenance, no timestamp, no signature, nothing that distinguishes 128 bits of physics from 32 bits of clock. Twelve words drawn from four billion possibilities are twelve perfectly ordinary words.

This is why the table of sources ranks each one by whether you can check it. The problem with a hardware generator is not that it is bad. It is that you cannot audit it. Dice are not more random than a sound hardware generator. They are more accountable, and every case above is an argument about accountability rather than about randomness.

A phrase handed to you by a device you cannot see inside is a trust decision. It may be a perfectly good one, made about a reputable maker with a published design and a supply chain you have reason to believe in. It is still a decision about whom to trust, and not something you can check by looking at the words.

Two caveats, in fairness. The figures above are the disclosing researchers' estimates rather than audited totals, and the amounts stolen are lower bounds, because they count only what could be traced. More importantly, this is a list of the failures that were found.

07 Doing it by hand

A six-sided die doesn't produce whole bits, so use rejection sampling: read 1 2 3 4 as 00 01 10 11 and reroll 5 and 6. Each accepted roll is exactly two bits with no bias from the mapping.

Coins are simpler at one flip per bit. If you doubt the coin is fair, flip in pairs and use von Neumann's trick: HT is 1, TH is 0, and matching pairs are discarded. Recipe B walks the procedure.

Write the bits out in groups of eleven. Each group is a word index from 0 to 2047, and on this poster you read it off directly: the high bits select the column, the low bits the row. The first eleven words of a 12-word phrase come straight from your first 121 bits.

The last word is where a hand process stops. Seven of your remaining entropy bits and four checksum bits share the final word, and those four bits are SHA-256 output. You cannot compute SHA-256 by hand in any practical sense.

So the honest procedure is: generate the entropy physically, then use an offline machine or a hardware wallet that accepts raw entropy or dice input to compute the final word. Several wallets support exactly this. The machine learns your entropy either way. The point of doing it by hand is that the machine did not choose it.

The roller runs this whole procedure inside the page, and its worksheet PDF puts the same steps on paper for a table with no computer on it.

Whatever you wrote the bits on is the phrase. Treat that worksheet as the secret it is: destroy it once the phrase is recorded and verified, and never photograph it. Do not reuse a set of rolls for a second wallet.

08 Threat model and hygiene

What a printed word list is. The public BIP-39 English list, identical in every wallet that implements the standard. Hanging it on a wall reveals no more than hanging up a dictionary. It earns its place, because it turns bits into words, gives you the four-letter prefixes hardware wallets ask for, and lets you confirm a word exists before you trust it to metal.

What it is not. Not a backup, not a secret, and not a verification tool, because checking a phrase needs a hash. Marking it changes all of that at once. A poster with twelve pencil ticks is your recovery phrase in plaintext, legible from across a room. Trace words on a separate sheet and destroy it afterwards.
