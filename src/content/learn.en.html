Entropy · a field guide

Twelve words are
a number

Everything that keeps that number yours is decided in the instant it is generated, before you write anything down and before you pick a wallet. This page shows the arithmetic rather than asserting it, for someone who has heard the word entropy without being told what it measures.

The short version

  • Entropy counts how many numbers yours could have been. It measures how you picked it, never how random the words look.
  • The list holds 2048 words because that is two to the eleventh, so one word is exactly eleven bits.
  • The checksum catches about fifteen copying mistakes in sixteen and says nothing about how guessable your phrase is.
  • Never take randomness from your own head, because words you choose are worth a few dozen bits.
  • Every wallet emptied by weak randomness was emptied because the number was small. The standard held every time.

Warning

Never mark your own words on a printed word list. Twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is a plaintext copy of your recovery phrase, and an easier one to read than your handwriting.

Never type a real recovery phrase into any website, including this one. This page has no field that accepts a phrase, makes no network requests and stores nothing, and you can check that by reading the source. Anything offering to “validate” or “recover” your phrase online is a theft pattern.

01 What a seed phrase is

A BIP-39 phrase is a number written in words. Each word is an index into a fixed, public, ordered list of 2048 entries, so abandon is 0 and zoo is 2047. Nothing else about a word matters: not its meaning, not its length, not whether it sounds like a strong password.

Your wallet does not store the words. It joins them, runs PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512 over the string for 2048 iterations, and gets a 512-bit seed from which every key is derived deterministically. The same words produce the same keys on any conforming wallet, forever, and that portability is why the standard exists.

So a phrase is worth exactly as much as its number is unpredictable. The word list, the derivation, the arithmetic and this poster are all public. Only the number was ever secret.

02 Entropy, defined properly

Entropy is not a measure of how random something looks. It measures how many equally likely possibilities a secret was drawn from, expressed as a base-2 logarithm so it can be counted in bits. n bits means 2^n equally likely candidates, and each extra bit doubles the work an attacker must do.

A 12-word phrase generated correctly carries 128 bits, so it was drawn from 2^128 candidates, about 3.4 × 10^38. Two handles on a number that size, each with its working shown.

  • Bitcoin's entire mining network. The largest purpose-built computing effort that has ever existed runs on the order of 10^21 hashes per second. At that rate, merely counting to 2^128 takes about 11 billion years, roughly the present age of the universe. A bare hash is far cheaper than one full trial of a phrase, so this underestimates the real cost.
  • Every computer on Earth. Suppose ten billion devices each test a billion candidates per second. That is 10^19 per second, and 2^128 still takes about 1.1 trillion years, some 78 times the age of the universe.

For honesty's sake, 2^128 is not enormous by every measure. That many water molecules is about ten cubic kilometres of water. What makes 128 bits enough is that no physical process can visit that many states one at a time.

Randomness is a property of the process, not of the output. From a fair source the bit string 0000…0000 is exactly as likely as any other, so a phrase that “looks random” tells you nothing, and one that looks meaningful is not thereby weak. Entropy is measured against what an attacker knows about your process. Dice give you 128 bits, and words that mean something to you are worth however many bits it takes to describe your habit of choosing.

03 Why 2048 words

2048 is 2^11, and that is the whole reason for the number. Eleven bits address exactly 2048 entries with nothing left over, so converting bits to words is a straight slice of the bit string: take eleven bits, read a word, repeat. A list of 2000 words would not divide the space evenly, and selecting from it without bias would need rejection sampling.

It is also why the poster can be a grid whose coordinates are the index. hamster is 837, which is 01101000101 in eleven bits.

Twelve words therefore carry 12 × 11 = 132 bits. BIP-39 does not spend all 132 on entropy. It generates ENT bits of randomness, appends ENT ÷ 32 checksum bits, and splits the result into eleven-bit groups. For twelve words that is 128 bits of entropy plus a 4-bit checksum; for twenty-four, 256 plus 8.

The last column is a consequence worth knowing: the final word is never free. With the first eleven fixed, only 128 of the 2048 words complete a valid 12-word phrase, so a sequence written down at random is valid about one time in sixteen.

Whether 12 or 24 words is better is not settled by entropy alone, because 128 bits is already beyond brute force by the margins above. 24 words is chiefly a hedge against future cryptanalysis and against Grover-style quantum search, which in theory halves the effective strength of a symmetric secret. Whether that speedup is ever practical is disputed, and longer phrases cost you more to record and to check.

04 The checksum

The checksum bits are the first ENT ÷ 32 bits of SHA-256 of the raw entropy, appended before the phrase is cut into words. That is why the last word carries a mixture of entropy and checksum rather than sitting apart.

What it catches: a word mistyped into some other real word, two words in the wrong order, a word read off the wrong row. Any single such mistake survives only if it happens to produce the right four bits, about one time in sixteen, so roughly fifteen errors in sixteen are caught. For 24 words the figure is 255 in 256.

What it does not catch matters more. It is not a security check, since a phrase assembled from twelve words you chose yourself passes exactly as readily as one from dice. It does not slow an attacker down, because they enumerate entropy values and compute the checksum exactly as the standard prescribes. And it does not tell you the phrase is yours, because a valid phrase that is not yours restores a real, empty wallet. “My wallet accepted it” says nothing about how guessable it is.

05 Where the randomness comes from

A person is not a random number generator, and this is not a matter of trying harder. Asked for a random digit, people over-choose 7 and shy away from 0 and 5. Asked for a sequence they avoid repeats and long runs, because repetition feels non-random, and asked for words they reach for concrete nouns, things in the room, names and dates. Every one of those tendencies is a pattern an attacker can rank candidates by, and an attacker who ranks does not have to enumerate.

The arithmetic is stark. Suppose you were unusually disciplined and chose each of your twelve words uniformly at random from a shortlist of the 16 you find most memorable. That is 4 bits per word and 48 bits for the phrase, against the 128 the format implies. Widen the shortlist to 32 words and you reach 60 bits, which a serious hardware budget still works through. The words look identical either way.

Which source to use is a practical question, and Start here answers it with a table and a procedure. The short version: a hardware wallet by default, dice if you want to audit every bit, and never your own head.

06 When entropy failed

Everything above this point is arithmetic. What follows is the record. In every case the software worked: keys were valid, transactions confirmed, and where there was a phrase the checksum passed. Nobody could have caught any of it by studying their own words. Five mechanisms are worth walking through, and the table below carries the full list with the numbers.

  • Debian's OpenSSL, 2006 to 2008. A maintainer removed two lines that fed uninitialised memory into the random pool, which Valgrind had correctly flagged. The only varying input left was the process id, and Linux hands out at most 32,768, so every key came from fifteen bits (CVE-2008-0166). The keys already existed and had been copied elsewhere, so patching fixed nothing and Debian shipped blacklists. A careful change, made for a good reason, that took the randomness out and left everything working.
  • Brainwallets, from around 2011. A passphrase you invented went through a single unsalted SHA-256 and became the private key, so the whole mapping could be precomputed. The 2016 Financial Cryptography study found 884 funded brainwallets and all but 21 had been drained, half within 21 minutes of funding. A human-chosen passphrase is worth about 20 bits.
  • Repeated signing nonces, a pattern rather than an incident. Two signatures, one nonce, and the key falls out. This one leaves its evidence in the open, because a signature sits in a block forever. Brengel and Rossow found 1,068 nonces reused across 4,433 keys, enough to have taken 412.80 BTC, and Breitner and Heninger computed 1,296 keys the same way. Every one of those addresses was already empty.
  • Milk Sad, 2023. Libbitcoin Explorer's bx seed used that same twister seeded with 32 bits of system clock. Ask it for 256 bits and it returned 256, all of it one clock reading stretched to fill the space. The output passed every test a user could apply, because the shortfall was in the input. The name is what it emits when the clock reads zero (CVE-2023-39910).
  • Preloaded and tampered devices. The crude version puts the phrase in the box. The thorough version is inside the device. A counterfeit taken apart in 2023 had its readout protection disabled and its bootloader checks stripped, and its firmware substituted one of 20 stored phrases for the one it appeared to generate. Every part of the ceremony was intact. The owner watched a device generate a phrase, wrote it down, and it was valid, and the checksum passed, and it was never theirs.

The three 32-bit cases deserve the arithmetic spelled out. 32 bits doesn't sound small until you count it. 2^32 is 4294967296, about 4.3 billion. The previous section put a single machine at 2^40 in a fortnight. 2^32 is 256 times smaller than that, so the same machine finishes the entire space in a little over an hour. The published estimates agree. A couple of hours on one computer for the Trust Wallet phrases. Under ten hours on a laptop to precompute every key Profanity could produce. At most a few days on a gaming machine for Libbitcoin's. That is not a hardware budget. It is an afternoon.

None of this was a failure of BIP-39. Wherever the standard was in the picture, it held. The word list was the same public 2048 words, correctly indexed. The derivation was correct. The checksum was correct, and it passed every time, because a well-formed phrase is the only thing it was ever asked to certify. What failed was the number underneath, and the number is the one part of the system nobody can inspect afterwards. A phrase does not record its own origin. There is no field in it for provenance, no timestamp, no signature, nothing that distinguishes 128 bits of physics from 32 bits of clock. Twelve words drawn from four billion possibilities are twelve perfectly ordinary words.

This is why the section on sources prefers what can be audited, and why it says of hardware generators that the problem is not that they are bad but that you cannot check them. Dice are not more random than a sound hardware generator. They are more accountable, and every case above is an argument about accountability rather than about randomness. A phrase handed to you by a device you cannot see inside is a trust decision. It may be a perfectly good one, made about a reputable maker with a published design and a supply chain you have reason to believe in. It is still a decision about whom to trust, and not something you can check by looking at the words.

Two caveats, in fairness. Most of the figures above are estimates by the researchers who found the flaw rather than audited totals, and the amounts stolen are lower bounds, because they count only what could be traced. More importantly, this is a list of the failures that were found. A generator that is merely predictable to whoever built it, and never exploited loudly enough to draw attention, leaves no trace in a record like this one.

07 Doing it by hand

Dice. A six-sided die does not produce whole bits, so use rejection sampling: read 1 2 3 4 as 00 01 10 11 and reroll 5 and 6. Each accepted roll is exactly two bits with no bias from the mapping, so 128 bits needs 64 accepted rolls and about 96 throws.

Coins. One flip, one bit, so 128 flips for a 12-word phrase. If you doubt the coin is fair, use von Neumann's trick: flip in pairs, read HT as 1 and TH as 0, and discard HH and TT. That removes any fixed bias for about four flips per bit.

Write the bits out in groups of eleven. Each group is a word index from 0 to 2047, and on this poster you read it off directly: the high bits select the column, the low bits the row. The first eleven words of a 12-word phrase come straight from your first 121 bits.

The last word is where a hand process stops. Seven of your remaining entropy bits and four checksum bits share the final word, and those four bits are SHA-256 output. You cannot compute SHA-256 by hand in any practical sense. So the honest procedure is: generate the entropy physically, then use an offline machine or a hardware wallet that accepts raw entropy or dice input to compute the final word. Several wallets support exactly this. The machine learns your entropy either way. The point of doing it by hand is that the machine did not choose it.

Whatever you wrote the bits on is the phrase. Treat that worksheet as the secret it is: destroy it once the phrase is recorded and verified, and never photograph it. Do not reuse a set of rolls for a second wallet.

08 Threat model and hygiene

What a printed word list is. The public BIP-39 English list, identical in every wallet that implements the standard. There is nothing sensitive about it, and hanging it on a wall reveals no more than hanging up a dictionary. It turns bits into words, it gives you the four-letter prefixes hardware wallets ask for, and it lets you confirm a word exists before you trust it to a metal plate.

What it is not. Not a backup, not a secret, and not a verification tool, since checking a phrase needs a hash. Marking it changes all of that at once. A poster with twelve pencil ticks is your recovery phrase in plaintext, legible from across a room. If you need to trace words while transcribing, use a separate sheet and destroy it afterwards.
