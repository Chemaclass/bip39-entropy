The short version

  • A seed phrase is a number written as words, and whoever knows the number owns the coins.
  • Its strength was decided the instant it was created, and nothing you do afterwards adds any.
  • No test on a finished phrase tells you it was generated well, so the process behind it is the only evidence you ever get.
  • Let a hardware wallet generate it, or throw dice if you want to check every bit yourself, and never pick the words yourself.
  • Offline is a fact about hardware, not a setting, and your phone is never offline in the sense that matters.
  • Write the words on paper by hand, restore from that paper before you fund the wallet, and never photograph or type them.

Before anything else

This page never asks for a recovery phrase, and no legitimate tool ever will. Not a wallet maker, not an exchange, not a support agent, not a “validator” or a “recovery service”. When someone asks for your words, the request is the attack.

Nothing here has a field that takes a phrase, and nothing here talks to a network. Read the source, or pull the cable, and check.

01 In one minute

A seed phrase is a number written as words. Twelve words are a 128-bit number plus four bits of checksum that catch typing mistakes. The words carry nothing by themselves, because each one is an entry in a public list of 2048. The same list in every wallet, and the same list printed on the poster.

Whoever knows that number owns the coins, and nothing else counts. Not the person holding the device, not the person who bought them, not the person whose name is on anything. There is no account to recover and no support desk that can step in from either direction.

So one thing stands between your coins and everyone else, which is that nobody can guess the number. Whether that holds was decided the instant the phrase was created, before you wrote anything down and before you chose a wallet.

02 Where the number comes from

A phrase generated properly is one of 2^128 possibilities, and nothing reaches that. An attacker doesn't need to break any mathematics, only to search a set smaller than you thought you were using. That has already emptied wallets at scale, through generators seeded from the clock and through passphrases people invented themselves, and in every case the software worked and the phrase looked ordinary. You can't look at twelve words and tell. So every option below asks you to trust something, and the question is what you are trusting and whether you can check it.

The realistic threats to a first-time owner are ordinary malware and ordinary mistakes.
  • Dice and coins are the only auditable option, because you watch every bit arrive. Hand-generated entropy is checkable rather than more random.
  • Choosing words yourself has no defence at all. Twelve words picked uniformly from thirty-two you find memorable is sixty bits, and a serious hardware budget works through sixty bits.

03 A recipe you can follow

The first recipe is what most people should do. The second is for the person who wants to watch every bit of their own entropy arrive. The order matters in both, above all the restore test, which comes before the money rather than after it.

Recipe A · A hardware wallet. The default.

  1. 1 · Buy it from the maker, or from a reseller they list themselves. If the box arrives with a recovery phrase already written inside, the device is a trap. The same goes for a phrase handed to you by a friend, a setup service, a tutorial or a support agent: you cannot know how many copies exist, so treat it as already compromised.
  2. 2 · Check it before it holds anything. Run the maker's authenticity check and read the firmware version off the device's screen rather than the computer's. A device that asks you to install software from a link you were sent isn't the device you ordered.
  3. 3 · Let the device generate the phrase. The words appear on its screen, where they should stay. They should never appear on your computer, at any point.
  4. 4 · Write them down by hand. Paper, pen, numbered one to twelve, spelled exactly as shown, and clear enough for a stranger to read.
  5. 5 · Confirm from your copy, not from memory. Read the words back off the paper in front of you, since this is the only moment when a copying error is cheap to find.
  6. 6 · Restore before you fund it. Wipe the device and restore it from your written copy alone. This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters.
  7. 7 · Send a small amount first. Receive a little, then send some back out so you know you can spend. Only then move the rest.

Recipe B · Dice and this poster. Slower, and auditable end to end. The roller takes the throws one by one and prints a worksheet, so you can work at a table with no computer at all.

  1. 1 · Count the throws. Twelve words is 128 bits, or 64 accepted throws of one die, and since fifths and sixths get discarded you should expect about 96 throws.
  2. 2 · Set the room up. Fresh paper, a die you have looked at, no phone on the table, nobody reading over your shoulder.
  3. 3 · Throw and record, in order. Read 1 2 3 4 as 00 01 10 11, while a 5 or 6 records nothing and gets thrown again, which is what keeps the four outcomes equally likely. Never “fix” a throw you don't like.
  4. 4 · Cut the bits into words. Each group of eleven bits is an index from 0 to 2047. On the poster you read it straight off, because the high bits pick the column and the low bits pick the row.
  5. 5 · Let a machine finish the last word. The twelfth word mixes your remaining bits with four checksum bits from SHA-256, which you can't compute by hand. The machine learns your entropy either way, and the point is that it did not choose it.
  6. 6 · Restore, then destroy the worksheet, because until you do, that worksheet is a second copy of your seed in longhand. Then the small amount first, exactly as in Recipe A.

04 Offline, and staying that way

Most people should stop at Recipe A and let a hardware wallet do the work. That is the recommendation rather than a hedge. The rest of this section is for the person generating entropy by hand, and for anyone about to print.

An air gap is a fact about hardware, not a setting, because settings get changed by software, updates, sleep cycles and people. Unplug the cable, then disable wifi and bluetooth separately, since bluetooth is a network and a paired phone is a route to the internet. Airplane mode is a pause rather than a property.

Your phone is never offline in the sense that matters. It synchronises, backs itself up and indexes its own contents, so a photograph taken in airplane mode uploads itself the moment the connection returns. That is how most phrases stored on phones leave the building, and your daily laptop is the same machine with a bigger screen.

To get this page off the network: save it with the “Save offline” control, compare its SHA-256 against the index.html.sha256 beside it, carry it across on removable media, and disconnect before you open it.

Staying offline is the harder half, because a modern computer remembers things for you and hands them over later. Screenshots land in synchronised albums, clipboard managers keep a scrollback, and shell history records arguments. A live session booted from a USB stick with persistence off removes most of that in one move.

Printing is the point of this project, and a printer is a computer you have never thought of as one. That costs you nothing here, because the poster is the public BIP-39 word list and the blank worksheet holds no results, so print both wherever is convenient. The dangerous sheet is the one you filled in, which is your seed in longhand, so write that by hand and never print it.

This page is one file with no network code, so the procedure above is possible to check as well as carry out. The build enforces that rather than the prose promising it.

05 After you have the words

Restore before you fund. Both recipes said it, and here it is a third time, because a backup you have never restored is a guess about the future. The moment to find out that a word is unreadable is while the wallet is empty.

Paper or steel. Paper written by hand costs nothing, though fire and water are the two things most likely to happen to a house, and stamped metal survives both for an hour of work. The backup should be worth about as much trouble as the amount it protects. Store it somewhere dry and private, and not beside the device.

Four things never to do, all the same mistake in different clothes.

  • Never photograph it. Files on modern devices copy themselves to a service by default, get indexed, and outlive the moment you took them.
  • Never type it anywhere, except into the wallet you are restoring on purpose, because keyboards remember and autocorrect learns.
  • Never store it in a password manager or a cloud note. Both are servers built to be reachable from anywhere, behind one password that now guards everything you own.
  • Never split it in a scheme you invented. Six known words of a twelve-word phrase leave about sixty-two bits, which a large hardware budget can reach, and home-made splits lose coins far more often than they prevent theft.

That last one always sounds careful, so here is why it isn't. The scheme lives in one head, and most coins that are lost are lost because nobody could reach them. A single well-hidden backup you can definitely find beats a clever system you can only mostly reconstruct.
