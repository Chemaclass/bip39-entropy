The short version

  • A seed phrase is a number written as words, and whoever knows the number owns the coins.
  • Its strength was decided the instant it was created, and nothing you do afterwards adds any.
  • No test on a finished phrase tells you it was generated well, so the process behind it is the only evidence you ever get.
  • Let a hardware wallet generate it, or throw dice if you want to check every bit yourself, and never pick the words yourself.
  • Offline is a fact about hardware, not a setting, and your phone is never offline in the sense that matters.
  • Write the words on paper by hand, restore from that paper before you fund the wallet, and never photograph or type them.

Before anything else

This page never asks for a recovery phrase. No legitimate tool ever will. Not a wallet maker, not an exchange, not a support agent, not a “validator” or a “recovery service”. No stranger ever needs your words. The request is the attack. Every time.

Nothing here has a field that takes a phrase. Nothing here talks to a network. Read the source, or pull the cable, and check.

01 In one minute

A seed phrase is a number written as words. Twelve words are a 128-bit number, plus four bits of checksum that catch typing mistakes. The words carry nothing by themselves. Each one is an entry in a public list of 2048. The same list in every wallet. The same list printed on the poster.

Whoever knows that number owns the coins. Not the person holding the device. Not the person who bought them. Not the person whose name is on anything. There is no account, no password reset, no support desk that can step in, in either direction. Lose the number and the coins are gone. Someone else learns it and they are gone the same way.

One thing stands between your coins and everyone else: nobody can guess the number. That isn't a metaphor. It's the entire security model. Whether it holds was decided the instant the phrase was created. Before you wrote anything down. Before you chose a wallet. Before you thought about where to hide the paper. Everything else on this page follows from that.

Everything here is stated, not proved. When you are starting, the procedure matters more than the arithmetic. The field guide does the proving: what entropy measures, why the list holds exactly 2048 words, and what the checksum can and can't tell you.

02 Why the randomness is the whole game

A phrase generated properly is one of 2^128 equally likely possibilities. That's about 3.4 × 10^38. Here is one handle on that number. Bitcoin's mining network is the largest purpose-built computing effort that has ever existed. It runs on the order of 10^21 hashes per second. At that rate, counting to 2^128 takes 3.4 × 10^17 seconds. That's about 11 billion years, roughly the present age of the universe. Nobody is guessing your phrase.

But that figure describes a phrase drawn uniformly from all 2^128. It says nothing about a phrase drawn from a smaller set. An attacker doesn't have to break any mathematics to make money. They have to search a set smaller than you thought you were using.

This isn't hypothetical. It's the one failure that has cost people money at scale. Two versions of it keep coming back.

  • Secrets that a person chose. Before seed phrases were standard, people derived a key from a passphrase they invented themselves. The practice was called a brainwallet. Attackers generated keys from song lyrics, scripture, book titles, quotations and password dumps, then swept the funds. Some balances were emptied seconds after they arrived, because the addresses were already precomputed and being watched. Human choice is a small space, however original the result feels from the inside.
  • Generators that were broken. Some software seeds its randomness from something predictable, like the clock or a fixed value. The phrases it makes look perfectly ordinary and sit in a set small enough to list out. It has happened again and again, in wallet programs and in the platform libraries under them. Nothing about the output looks wrong. You can't look at twelve words and tell.

That last point deserves its own paragraph. No test on a finished phrase tells you whether it was generated well. A wallet accepting it proves the checksum matches, and a hand-picked phrase matches too. The only evidence you will ever have about a phrase's strength is what you know about the process that produced it. So the rest of this page is about process.

03 Where randomness can come from

Every option asks you to trust something. The question isn't which one is perfect. It's what you are trusting, and whether you can check it. The table is the summary, a word per cell. The honest version of each row comes after.

Sources of randomness
Source How it works Can you check it What goes wrong Verdict
Hardware wallet Sealed chip No Firmware Default
Dice Your throws Every bit Fumbling Auditable
Coins Your flips Every bit Miscounting Auditable
OS generator Kernel pool Code only The machine Offline
Browser tab Asks the OS The page The browser Learning
You choose You pick Nothing Too few bits Never

Every verdict in that table has a catch. The catches are where people go wrong.

  • A hardware wallet's own generator is the right answer for almost everyone. You also can't audit it. Both halves are true. You are trusting a chip, a firmware build, and the route the box travelled to reach you. In exchange, the phrase is made inside a device designed to keep it. It never appears on a general-purpose computer. It never crosses a screen that something else might be reading. That trade is a good one. The realistic threats to a first-time owner are ordinary malware and ordinary mistakes, not a compromised chip.
  • Dice and coins can be audited, which is rare, and it is the only reason to prefer them. You watch every bit arrive. They are also slow and easy to fumble: a die with rounded edges, a throw written in the wrong box, a worksheet left on a desk, a phone in the room. Hand-generated entropy is not more random than a sound hardware source. It's checkable. And only if you will stay careful for the full hour it takes.
  • An operating system's generator is good cryptography: /dev/urandom on Unix-like systems, the equivalent call elsewhere. It is reseeded constantly and reviewed hard. The weakness isn't the algorithm. It's that you are trusting the whole machine it runs on: the kernel, the drivers, whatever else is installed, whatever the machine has been exposed to. On a clean, offline system it's a strong choice. On your everyday laptop it's a guess about your everyday laptop.
  • A website or a browser tab calls that same operating system generator in the end, so the randomness is usually fine. Everything around it is the problem: extensions that can read the page, the page cache, memory written to disk, screenshots, session restore, and whether the copy you loaded is the copy the author wrote. Good for learning how this works. Not for making a phrase that will hold savings.
  • Choosing words yourself is the one option with no defence. Ask a person for random things and you get patterns: concrete nouns, things in the room, names, dates, no repeats. Say you disciplined yourself into picking twelve words uniformly from a shortlist of thirty-two you find memorable. That is sixty bits, and a serious hardware budget works through sixty bits. It isn't a weaker kind of security. It's the absence of it.

04 A recipe you can follow

Two procedures. The first is what most people should do. The second is for the person who wants to watch every bit of their own entropy arrive, and who will spend an evening on it. The order of the steps matters in both. Above all the restore test, which comes before the money, not after.

Recipe A · A hardware wallet. The default.

  1. 1 · Buy it from the maker. Order from the manufacturer, or from a reseller they list themselves. Not a marketplace listing, not an auction, not secondhand, not a gift from someone in a chat. If the box arrives with a recovery phrase already written on a card inside, the device is a trap. Stop and return it.
  2. 2 · Check the device before it holds anything. Run the manufacturer's own authenticity check. Install or confirm the firmware from their official channel. Read the firmware version off the device's own screen, not off the computer. A device that asks you to install software from a link you were sent isn't the device you ordered.
  3. 3 · Let the device generate the phrase. Pick the option that creates a new wallet. The words appear on the device's screen. They should never appear on your computer screen, at any point. Set a PIN when it asks.
  4. 4 · Write the words down by hand. Paper, pen, numbered one to twelve, in order, spelled exactly as shown. Write clearly enough that a stranger could read it, because in the worst case that is the point. Don't photograph the screen. Don't type the words anywhere.
  5. 5 · Confirm from your copy, not from memory. The device will ask you to re-enter some or all of the words. Read them off the paper in front of you. This is the only moment when a copying error is cheap to find. Reading from memory kills the check.
  6. 6 · Restore before you fund it. Wipe the device. Restore it from your written copy alone. If the same wallet comes back, your backup works. If it doesn't, you found that out for free. This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters.
  7. 7 · Send a small amount first. Receive a little. Confirm it arrives. Send some of it back out, so you know you can spend from this wallet. Only then move the rest.

Recipe B · Dice and this poster. Slower. Auditable end to end. The roller takes the throws one by one, and prints a worksheet so you can do it at a table with no computer at all.

  1. 1 · Decide the length and the count. Twelve words is 128 bits. That is 64 accepted throws of one six-sided die. Fifths and sixths get discarded, so expect about 96 throws in total.
  2. 2 · Set the room up. Fresh paper, a die you have looked at, no phone on the table, no camera in the room, nobody reading over your shoulder. Print the worksheet first if you are working away from the screen. Print the poster too, or have it on the wall.
  3. 3 · Throw and record, in order. Read 1 2 3 4 as 00 01 10 11. A 5 or a 6 records nothing and gets thrown again. Discarding them is what keeps the four outcomes equally likely. Never “fix” a throw you don't like. Never reuse a set of throws for a second wallet.
  4. 4 · Cut the bits into words. Write the bits out in groups of eleven. Each group is an index from 0 to 2047. On the poster you read it straight off: the high bits pick the column, the low bits pick the row. For a twelve-word phrase, the first eleven words come entirely from your own bits.
  5. 5 · Let a machine finish the last word. The twelfth word mixes your remaining entropy bits with four checksum bits from SHA-256, and you can't compute that by hand. Enter your bits into an offline machine, or into a hardware wallet that takes dice input or raw entropy. The machine learns your entropy either way. The point is that it did not choose it.
  6. 6 · Record the finished phrase. Numbered, in order, by hand, the same as Recipe A step 4.
  7. 7 · Restore, then destroy the worksheet. Load the phrase into the wallet you plan to use, offline, and confirm the addresses. Then burn or shred every sheet of bits. Until you do, that worksheet is a second copy of your seed in longhand.
  8. 8 · Small amount first, then fund it. As above. Receive, spend, confirm. Only then move anything you would miss.

05 Going offline

Most people reading this should stop at Recipe A and let a hardware wallet do the work. That is not a hedge. That is the recommendation. What follows is for two narrower cases. You have decided to generate the entropy yourself, and you want the machine involved to do as little as possible. Or you are about to print something, and you would rather the printing step did not undo the care you took everywhere else.

An air gap is a fact about hardware, not a setting. It means a machine with no path to a network at all. Not a machine whose network is switched off right now. Settings get changed by software, updates, sleep cycles, docking stations and people. Take the wireless card out, or use a machine that never had one. That is a different kind of claim.

Turning the network off, for real. Unplug the ethernet cable. Then disable wifi and bluetooth, separately. Bluetooth is a network, and a paired phone is a route to the internet. Airplane mode is a pause, not a property. Plenty of devices switch the radios back on when they wake, when they reboot, or when an update decides it needs the connection. If the machine won't stay off, use a different machine.

Your phone is never offline in the sense that matters. It is built to synchronise. It backs itself up. It restores itself onto new devices. It indexes its own contents so you can search them. It saves copies of what is on screen. It runs software you didn't choose and can't list. A photograph taken in airplane mode uploads itself the moment the connection returns. That is how most phrases stored on phones leave the building. Your daily laptop is the same machine with a bigger screen.

Getting this page off the network, in this order. Each step exists so the next one can happen with nothing listening.

  1. 1 · Save it to disk. The header has a “Save offline” control that writes this page out as a single file. It works because the page is self-contained. No fonts, images, scripts or styles come from anywhere else. The saved copy is the whole thing, not a shell that breaks the moment it can't reach a server.
  2. 2 · Check what you downloaded. Every deploy publishes the file's SHA-256 beside it, as index.html.sha256. Compare it against the file you saved before you trust the copy. Be clear about what that proves. The same server served both, so it catches a download that was truncated, cached or swapped in transit. It does not prove the server itself was honest. The strong version is cloning the repository and running its verify script, which rebuilds the page from source and diffs the result.
  3. 3 · Move it on removable media to the machine that will do the work, instead of downloading it there. A machine that never fetched anything has less to explain.
  4. 4 · Disconnect, then open it. Not the other way round. Open the local file with the network already down. At no point should a tab holding your entropy sit on a machine with a route to the outside world.
  5. 5 · Do the work, write the words by hand, and leave the machine that way until you have finished copying and checking. Reconnecting “to look something up” in the middle undoes the other four.

A clean environment. On a general-purpose computer, the strongest realistic option is a live session booted from a USB stick with persistence switched off. You get an operating system that has never been used, so nothing already installed on the machine is running. You get a session that exists only in memory, so nothing you do survives the reboot. That is the whole appeal. The cleanup is a power cycle, not a list of things to remember to delete.

The trade-offs are worth saying out loud, especially if you have never done it. You need a second working computer to prepare the stick. Check the image's signature or hash while you are still online, because a live session built from a tampered image is worse than not bothering. The machine may need its boot order or its secure boot settings changed. Printers and wifi may not work without drivers you would have to fetch, which is inconvenient by design. It does not fix hardware that is already compromised. It does not help if you reconnect halfway through. What it does is narrow the problem to the hardware and the image. That is a much smaller problem than the one you started with.

The browser is still the weakest link. Not because the randomness is bad, since the browser asks the operating system for that. It is everything around it. Extensions can read what is on the page. Pages get cached. Memory gets written to swap. Sessions get restored after a crash, sometimes with the contents intact. Clipboards are shared and read. A live session removes most of that in one move. That is why it is worth the trouble if you are going to use a browser at all.

So, plainly: generating a phrase for real money in a browser tab is the worst of the acceptable options. For nearly everyone, a hardware wallet is the better one. This page exists to teach the mechanism and to let you check it. That is a different job from holding savings.

This page is one file with no network code so that the procedure above is possible to carry out and possible to check. It loads nothing, sends nothing, stores nothing. The build enforces that, rather than the prose promising it. Read the source, or work with the cable pulled.

06 Staying offline

Going offline is a moment. Staying offline is the hard half. A modern computer is full of features whose whole job is to remember things for you and hand them to another machine later. None of them need a network at the time. They wait.

  • Screenshots and screen recording. A screenshot is a file in a folder, and that folder is very often a synchronised album. It uploads when the machine reconnects. Screen recording and meeting software can run with no visible window. If you wouldn't photograph the words, don't put them on a screen you haven't accounted for.
  • Clipboard history. Copying isn't temporary any more. Operating systems and clipboard managers keep a scrollback of everything copied, sometimes on disk, sometimes synchronised between your own devices. One copy-and-paste becomes a permanent record on hardware you weren't thinking about.
  • Shell history. Anything typed at a command line goes into a history file, arguments included. Pass a phrase or a set of raw entropy bits to a tool that way and it is now written to your home directory in plain text. Backed up with everything else.
  • Editors and their safety nets. Autosave, swap and recovery files, undo history, the recent-documents list. They all exist to survive a crash, which means they survive you closing the file without saving. Don't use a text editor as scratch paper for this. Use actual paper.
  • Voice assistants and always-on input. Read the words aloud near a listening microphone and you have made a transcript waiting to be uploaded. That includes phones and speakers in the room, not only the machine in front of you.
  • Sync folders. The desktop, the documents folder and the downloads folder are commonly synchronised. A file written there while you are offline isn't private. It's queued. It uploads the moment the machine comes back, and it won't ask you again.

Every item on that list is a reason to prefer the live session or the hardware wallet. On a machine with no persistent disk and no accounts signed in, most of these traps don't exist. There is nowhere for the remembered copy to live, and nothing to sync it to later.

Printing, which is the point of this project. A printer is a computer you have never thought of as one. Many keep a spool of recent jobs, and office models often hold them on an internal disk for a long time. Shared and networked printers keep job logs with the document name and the person who sent it. A printer shared over wifi is a network device by definition. The job crosses a network to reach it, and anything else on that network can reach it too.

The good news: this project's printed output is not secret. The poster is the public BIP-39 word list, shipped in every wallet, downloadable by anyone. Print it at work, at a copy shop, over any network you like. It reveals nothing. The blank worksheet is equally safe, because it holds boxes and instructions and no results. Print both wherever is convenient, and print them in advance.

The dangerous sheet is the one you filled in, or anything else carrying your words or your bits. That is your seed in longhand. It should never reach a printer. Write it by hand instead. If you must print something derived from a real phrase, use a printer wired straight to the machine doing the work, never a shared or network one, and assume the printer kept a copy.

Coming back online. People forget this step, because by then the interesting part is over.

  1. 1 · Finish the paper first. Copy the words, check the spelling, confirm the order, and finish any restore test before anything reconnects. Once the machine is online again, you shouldn't want to touch the phrase on it.
  2. 2 · Destroy the working copies. Burn or shred the sheets of bits. A filled-in worksheet isn't a draft. It's a duplicate of the secret, and it deserves the same ending as any other copy you didn't mean to keep.
  3. 3 · Power off the live session. This is the clean ending, and the reason to have used one. Cut the power to a session with no persistence and the whole environment goes at once. Nothing left to find, nothing left to remember to delete. Then wipe or destroy the media you carried the file on, and don't save that file back to any disk that persists.
  4. 4 · If you used an ordinary machine, assume it kept something. Walk the list above on purpose: clipboard history, shell history, editor recovery files, the downloads and desktop folders, the trash, the screenshots album. Then reconnect. If that list feels like too much to be sure about, take the hint. The secret should not have been on that machine. Generate the phrase again somewhere cleaner, while it still holds nothing.

07 After you have the words

Restore before you fund. Both recipes said it. Here it is a third time, because this is the step that gets skipped. A backup you have never restored is a guess about the future. The moment to find out that a word is unreadable or in the wrong position is while the wallet is empty.

Paper or steel. Paper written by hand is a good backup and costs nothing. Fire destroys it and water ruins it, and those are the two things most likely to happen to a house. Stamped or punched metal survives both, for an hour of work and a modest piece of equipment. A fair rule: the backup should be worth about as much trouble as the amount it protects. Paper while you are learning. Metal once losing it would hurt. Either way, store it somewhere dry, private, and not in the same place as the device.

Four things never to do, all the same mistake in different clothes.

  • Never photograph it. A photograph is a file. Files on modern devices copy themselves to a service by default, get indexed, get read by whatever software has permission to read photographs, and outlive the moment you took them.
  • Never type it into a phone or a computer, except into the wallet you are restoring on purpose, on a device you control. Keyboards remember. Notes applications synchronise. Autocorrect learns. Anything you type has passed through software you never audited.
  • Never store it in a password manager or a cloud note. Both are servers holding your data, behind one password that now guards everything you own. Both are built to be reachable from anywhere. That is the exact property you were trying to avoid.
  • Never split it in a clever scheme you invented. Half here and half there. Six words with a relative. A shift you will remember. These schemes are worse in both directions at once.

That last one needs its reason, because it always sounds careful. Splitting a phrase in half weakens each half far more than people expect. Six known words of a twelve-word phrase leave about sixty-two bits. That is not comfortably out of reach of a large hardware budget. Whoever finds one half has a real chance, not none.

Worse, home-made splits lose coins far more often than they prevent theft. The scheme lives in one head. Memory fades. People move house. Relatives die. The envelope goes out during a clear-out. The instructions turn out to be unclear to everyone except the person who wrote them, and that person is by then unavailable. Most coins that are lost are lost because nobody could reach them, not because somebody stole them. If you truly need to split custody, the established answers are multisignature wallets and standardised share schemes. Both are easy to get wrong, and both deserve reading about first. A single well-hidden backup you can definitely find beats a clever system you can only mostly reconstruct.

Those layers, the passphrase and multisignature, are worked through on Protect: what each one buys, what it costs, and how each one fails. Read it before you add either. A layer you have not rehearsed is a way to lose coins, not a way to keep them.

08 Common mistakes

Nearly every loss that wasn't a market loss is on this list.

  • Buying a device that arrives preloaded. A wallet with its recovery phrase already printed on a card in the box isn't a convenience. Whoever put it there knows the phrase, and they are waiting for the address to be funded. A genuine device generates its phrase in front of you, once, on its own screen.
  • Choosing the words by hand. Twelve words that mean something. Or that feel arbitrary. Or that came from opening a book at random. The result looks exactly like a good phrase and is catastrophically smaller as a search space.
  • Accepting a phrase somebody else produced. From a helpful friend, a setup service, a tutorial, a video, a support agent, a screenshot. You have no way to know how many copies exist. Treat any phrase you didn't watch being created as already compromised.
  • Testing the recovery only after funding. By then the test has stakes, so it doesn't get done. The first real restore attempt happens on the day something has already gone wrong. Restore while the wallet is empty and the exercise costs nothing.
  • Marking the words on a printed word list. Twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is a plaintext copy of your recovery phrase, and easier to read than your own handwriting. The poster is the public standard list, and hanging it up gives nothing away. Marking it is what turns it into a secret, and a badly protected one.

If you would rather know why this is true than take it on trust, the field guide works through the arithmetic: what entropy measures, why the list has exactly 2048 words, what the checksum does and does not catch, and what a chosen phrase is worth. None of it changes the procedure above. It means you are not taking anyone's word for it. That is the habit worth having here.
