Some of these get asked before a first phrase exists. Some get asked years later. Each answer is as short as the question allows and no shorter. Where an answer rests on arithmetic, the field guide shows the working. Where it rests on a procedure, start here gives the steps. The second half is a glossary. Most of the confusion around seed phrases is vocabulary, not mathematics.

The short version

  • Word order is part of the secret, so number your backup one to twelve and never sort it.
  • You don't get to choose the words: entropy measures how you picked, not how random the result feels.
  • A passphrase has nothing to check it against, so forgetting one loses the coins for good.
  • One missing word is usually recoverable offline, because you supply the other eleven, and that's recovery, not a shortcut.
  • Printing the word list is safe, but ticking your own words on it is a plaintext copy of your phrase.

Warning

One answer below explains how to recover a phrase that's missing a single word. That works only because you supply the other eleven and know where the gap is. It isn't a way in for anybody else. Nothing on this page shortens the search for a phrase you never knew.

No legitimate party ever needs your recovery phrase. Not a wallet maker, not an exchange, not a support agent, not a “validator” or a “recovery service”. This page has no field that accepts a phrase. It makes no network requests and stores nothing.

01 Questions

Roughly in the order they arrive. Where an answer depends on arithmetic, the field guide has the working. Where it depends on a procedure, start here has the steps.

  • Twelve words or twenty-four? Twelve is fine, and twenty-four is not paranoid. Twelve carry 128 bits and twenty-four carry 256, and the field guide shows why 128 is already past brute force, so twenty-four is not stronger against any attack that exists. It hedges against future cryptanalysis and Grover-style quantum search, which in theory halves a symmetric secret. Both lengths work everywhere, and the longer one costs more to record and keep legible. Without a reason to prefer one, write twelve.
  • Does the order of the words matter? Completely. The phrase is one number cut into eleven-bit groups, and each word is the group at a fixed position, so moving two words changes the number as surely as changing one. A swap will usually fail the checksum, which is a mercy rather than a safeguard, because about one such mistake in sixteen passes on a twelve-word phrase. Number your backup one to twelve as you write it, and never sort it.
  • Can I choose the words myself? No, and it is the most expensive mistake on this page. Entropy is measured against what an attacker knows about how you chose, not against how arbitrary the result feels from the inside. Twelve words picked uniformly from the thirty-two you find most memorable is sixty bits, which a serious hardware budget works through, and the result looks exactly like a good phrase.
  • What is a passphrase, the “twenty-fifth word”? An extra secret you supply alongside the words, mixed into the same derivation, so the same twelve words with a different passphrase produce a completely different wallet. It is taken literally, including case and spacing. What it buys is that the paper alone is no longer enough to spend. What it costs is that nobody can recover it: forget it and the coins are gone as completely as if you had burnt the phrase. Protect works through both halves.
  • I have lost one word. Am I finished? Probably not, provided you know which position is missing and the other eleven are right. There are only 2048 candidates, and the checksum leaves roughly 128 of them for a twelve-word phrase, so derive each and see which brings back addresses you recognise. For twenty-four words only about eight survive. Be clear about what this is: it works only because you already supply almost the whole secret, and somebody starting from nothing still faces all 2^128.
  • Is it safe to print or hang the whole word list? Yes. The poster is the public BIP-39 English list, identical in every wallet and downloadable by anyone, so hanging it reveals no more than hanging up a dictionary. Print it at work or at a copy shop over any network you like, and the blank worksheet is equally safe because it holds no results. What is dangerous is a marked list, because twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is your phrase in plaintext.
  • Can I keep the phrase in a password manager, a photo, or a cloud note? No, and all three are the same mistake wearing different clothes. Each turns a secret that lived in one physical place into a file on a server built to be reachable from anywhere. A password manager puts everything you own behind one password and somebody else's breach, and a photograph copies itself to a service by default. Write it by hand and keep it offline.
  • Will the same words work in a different wallet? Yes, and that portability is why the standard exists. What people hit instead is a restore that comes back empty, and the usual cause is the derivation path: one seed contains an unlimited tree of keys, and wallets read different branches by default. Find the address type and path the old wallet used rather than concluding the phrase is bad. A valid phrase that isn't yours also restores a real, empty wallet, so an empty wallet is a question, not an answer.
  • Should I split the phrase into halves and store them separately? Not in a scheme you invented. Six known words of a twelve-word phrase leave about sixty-two bits, which a large hardware budget can reach, so whoever finds one half has a real chance. Home-made splits also lose coins far more often than they prevent theft. If you need to split custody, the established answers are multisignature wallets and standardised share schemes, both covered in Protect.
  • How do I know this page is what it claims? Two checks, proving different amounts. The weaker one is the hash: every deploy publishes the file's SHA-256 as index.html.sha256, which catches a truncated or substituted download but says nothing about whether the server was honest. The stronger one is cloning the repository and running scripts/verify.sh. That rebuilds the page from source and diffs it against the committed file, checks the word list against the canonical BIP-39 hash, and fails if the page has gained any network-capable code. A published hash is a claim. A reproducible build is a check.
  • Does quantum computing change any of this? Not today. For the phrase itself the relevant result is Grover-style search, which in theory halves the effective strength of a symmetric secret and is the one real argument for twenty-four words. Whether that speedup is ever practical is disputed. The larger question isn't about your phrase at all: public-key signatures are the part a large quantum computer would threaten, and that is a protocol-level problem the whole network would meet together. You don't solve it by writing more words on paper.

One question isn't on this list, and it's asked too rarely: who else has seen this phrase? Treat any phrase you didn't watch being created as already compromised. That covers a helpful friend, a setup service, a tutorial, a video, and a card in the box. Roll your own exists so you can watch every bit of one arrive. Start here explains why a hardware wallet is still the right default for most people.

02 Words you will meet

Most of the confusion around seed phrases is vocabulary, not mathematics. These are the terms used on this site and in wallet software. The table gives a line per term. The five that don't fit in a line follow it. Entropy, bits and the checksum are worked through properly in the field guide. Air gaps and live sessions in start here.

Glossary
Term Meaning
Seed phraseA number written as words
EntropyUnpredictability, in bits
BitOne binary digit
ChecksumBits that catch a typo
BIP-39How bits become words
Word listThe public 2048 words
IndexA word's place, 0 to 2047
Derivation pathWhich branch of keys
PassphraseAn extra secret you add
Air gapNo network hardware
Live sessionA USB boot, nothing kept
Hardware walletA device that keeps keys
Brute forceTrying every candidate
Rejection samplingDiscarding rolls to stay fair

Five of those need a sentence rather than a line. Entropy counts how many equally likely secrets yours was drawn from. That's why it's measured in bits, and why it tells you nothing about how the result looks. A checksum catches a slip in copying and nothing else, so a wallet accepting a phrase is never evidence that the phrase is hard to guess. A derivation path picks one branch of the unlimited tree of keys that a single seed contains, which is why the same words can restore into two wallets that show you different addresses. A passphrase is an extra secret mixed into that derivation, and it's unrecoverable once forgotten. Rejection sampling is the habit of throwing out results that don't divide evenly, such as a 5 or a 6 on a six-sided die. It's what keeps the results you do keep equally likely.

Two of those carry more weight than the rest. Entropy is what everything else rests on, and the field guide spends a section on what it measures, because “looks random” is not it. A passphrase is powerful and unforgiving in equal measure. Read that answer in full before you decide to use one.
