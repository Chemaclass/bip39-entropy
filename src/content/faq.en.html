These are the ones asked before a first phrase exists and the ones asked years later, answered as briefly as each will allow and no more briefly than that. Where an answer rests on arithmetic, the field guide shows the working. Where it rests on a procedure, start here gives the steps. The second half is a glossary, because most of the confusion around seed phrases turns out to be vocabulary rather than mathematics.

Warning

One answer below describes how a phrase missing a single word can be recovered. That works only because you already supply the other eleven and know where the gap is. It is not a way in for anybody else, and nothing on this page shortens the search for a phrase you never knew.

No legitimate party ever needs your recovery phrase. Not a wallet maker, not an exchange, not a support agent, not a “validator” or a “recovery service”. This page has no field that accepts a phrase, makes no network requests and stores nothing.

01 Questions

Roughly in the order they arrive. Where an answer depends on arithmetic, the field guide has the working; where it depends on a procedure, start here has the steps.

  • Twelve words or twenty-four? Twelve words carry 128 bits of entropy and twenty-four carry 256. The field guide works through why 128 is already past what brute force can reach, so twenty-four is not stronger against any attack that exists. It is a hedge against future cryptanalysis and against Grover-style quantum search, which in theory halves the effective strength of a symmetric secret: 128 bits would become 64, and 256 would become 128. Whether that speedup is ever practical is genuinely disputed. Both lengths are supported by every conforming wallet, and the longer one costs you more to record, more to check and more to keep legible for decades. If you have no particular reason to prefer one, twelve is fine and twenty-four is not paranoid.
  • Does the order of the words matter? Completely. The phrase is one number cut into eleven-bit groups, and each word is the group at a fixed position, so moving two words changes the number as surely as changing one of them. There is no anagram and no unordered bag of twelve. A swap will usually fail the checksum, which is a mercy rather than a safeguard: about one such mistake in sixteen passes the check on a twelve-word phrase, and a phrase that passes but is not yours restores a real, empty wallet without complaint. Number your backup one to twelve as you write it, and never sort it.
  • Can I choose the words myself? No, and it is the most expensive mistake on this page. Entropy is measured against what an attacker knows about how you chose, not against how arbitrary the result feels from the inside, and human choice is a small space however hard you try. Even if you disciplined yourself into picking twelve words uniformly from a shortlist of the thirty-two you find most memorable, that is sixty bits, which a serious hardware budget works through. The checksum will not save you, because it checks transcription and nothing else. A hand-picked phrase whose last word happens to fit passes exactly as readily as one from dice, so “my wallet accepted it” tells you the arithmetic is consistent and never that the number is hard to guess.
  • What is a passphrase, the “twenty-fifth word”? It is an extra secret you supply alongside the words, mixed into the same derivation that turns them into a seed, so the same twelve words with a different passphrase produce a completely different wallet. It is taken literally, including case and spacing. What it buys is that the paper alone is no longer enough: somebody who finds your backup gets an empty wallet unless they also have the passphrase, which is worth a great deal against theft of the physical copy. What it costs is that there is nothing to check it against. Every passphrase is valid, so a wrong one raises no error, it simply opens another real and empty wallet, and no wallet, device or service anywhere records what you chose. A forgotten or mistyped passphrase loses the coins as completely as a lost phrase, and one that lives only in your head is a backup with no copy. Far more is lost this way than to burglars.
  • I have lost one word. Am I finished? Probably not, provided you know which position is missing and the other eleven are correct. There are only 2048 candidates for the gap, so a wallet or an offline recovery tool can simply try all of them. The checksum then does most of the work: for a twelve-word phrase it is four bits, so about one candidate in sixteen survives and roughly 128 phrases are left. Those are settled by deriving each one and seeing which brings back addresses you recognise from your own history. For a twenty-four-word phrase the checksum is eight bits and only about eight candidates survive. It is harder if you do not know the position, and harder again with two words missing, but both are still worth attempting offline before you give up. Be clear about what this is. It is recovery, and it works only because you already supply almost the entire secret. It is not a shortcut into anybody else's phrase, because somebody starting from nothing still faces all 2^128.
  • Is it safe to print or hang the whole word list? Yes. The poster is the public BIP-39 English list, identical in every wallet that implements the standard and downloadable by anyone, so hanging it on a wall reveals no more than hanging up a dictionary. Print it at work, at a copy shop, over any network you like. The blank worksheet is equally safe, because it holds boxes and instructions and no results. What is dangerous is marking your own words on it. Twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is a plaintext copy of your recovery phrase, easier to read from across a room than your own handwriting, and immune to the usual excuse that the photograph was only of the wall. If you need to trace words while transcribing, use a separate sheet and destroy it afterwards.
  • Can I keep the phrase in a password manager, a photo, or a note in the cloud? No, and all three are the same mistake wearing different clothes. Each turns a secret that existed in one physical place into a file on a server designed to be reachable from anywhere, which is precisely the property you were trying to avoid. A password manager puts everything you own behind one password and somebody else's breach. A photograph is a file that modern devices copy to a service by default, index, and hand to whatever software has permission to read photographs, and one taken in airplane mode uploads itself the moment the connection returns. A note in a synced folder written while offline is not private, it is queued. Even typing the words is a risk of its own, because keyboards learn, clipboard history is kept and often synchronised, and shell and editor files remember what you deleted. Write it by hand, and type it only into the wallet you are deliberately restoring.
  • Will the same words work in a different wallet? Yes, and that portability is the reason the standard exists. The same phrase produces the same seed, and therefore the same keys, on any conforming wallet, forever. What people actually hit is a restore that comes back empty, and the usual cause is the derivation path. One seed contains an unlimited tree of keys, and each wallet reads particular branches of it by default, so two wallets that prefer different branches will show you two different sets of addresses from identical words and neither is wrong. If a restore looks empty, do not conclude the phrase is bad. Find the address type and derivation path the old wallet used, and point the new one at it. Remember too that a valid phrase which is not yours restores a real, empty wallet without complaint, so an empty wallet is a question rather than an answer.
  • Should I split the phrase into halves and store them separately? Not one you invented. The arithmetic is worse than it sounds. Six known words of a twelve-word phrase leave about sixty-two bits, which is not comfortably out of reach of a large hardware budget, so whoever finds one half has a real chance rather than none. You have not halved the attacker's work, you have handed over half the secret and left the remainder thin. The larger problem runs the other way. Home-made splits lose coins far more often than they prevent theft, because the scheme lives in one head. Memory fades, people move house, relatives die, the envelope is thrown out during a clear-out, and the instructions turn out to be ambiguous to everyone except the person who wrote them, who is by then unavailable. Most coins that are lost are lost through inaccessibility, not robbery. If you genuinely need to split custody, the established answers are multisignature wallets and standardised share schemes, both of which are also easy to get wrong and deserve reading about first.
  • How do I know this page is what it claims? Two checks exist and they prove different amounts. The weaker one is the hash: every deploy publishes the file's SHA-256 beside it as index.html.sha256, so you can compare it against the copy you saved before you trust that copy. It catches a truncated, cached or substituted-in-transit download, and it does not prove the server was honest, because the same server served both. The stronger one is to clone the repository and run scripts/verify.sh, which rebuilds index.html from the sources and diffs it against the committed file, checks the word list against the canonical BIP-39 hash, confirms all 2048 words reached the page, and fails if the page has gained any network-capable code. A reproducible build is a claim you can check for yourself rather than one you take on trust. Beyond that the page is a single file that loads nothing and sends nothing, which you can confirm by reading it or by working with the cable pulled.
  • Does quantum computing change any of this? Not today, and the honest answer has two halves. For the phrase itself the relevant result is Grover-style search, which in theory halves the effective strength of a symmetric secret, taking 128 bits to 64 and 256 to 128. Whether that speedup is ever practical is genuinely disputed, and it is the one real argument for twenty-four words. The larger question is not about your phrase at all. Public-key signatures are the part that a sufficiently large quantum computer would threaten, and that is a protocol-level problem the whole network would meet together rather than something you solve by writing more words on paper. No machine capable of either exists now, and one becoming plausible would be visible for years beforehand rather than arriving overnight. Generating your phrase from a source you trust and keeping the paper safe are still the two decisions that determine whether you keep your coins.

One question that is not here is asked too rarely: who else has seen this phrase? Any phrase you did not watch being created should be treated as already compromised, whether it came from a helpful friend, a setup service, a tutorial, a video, or a card in the box. Roll your own exists so that you can watch every bit of one arrive, and start here explains why a hardware wallet is nonetheless the right default for most people.

02 Words you will meet

Most of the confusion around seed phrases is vocabulary rather than mathematics. These are the terms used on this site and in wallet software. The table is a summary, a line per term; the five that cannot be said in a line follow it. Entropy, bits and the checksum are worked through properly in the field guide; air gaps and live sessions in start here.

Glossary
Term Meaning
Seed phraseA number written as words
EntropyUnpredictability, in bits
BitOne binary digit
ChecksumBits that catch a typo
BIP-39Bits to words: the rules
Word listThe public 2048 words
IndexA word's place, 0 to 2047
Derivation pathWhich branch of keys
PassphraseAn extra secret you add
Air gapNo network hardware
Live sessionA USB boot, nothing kept
Hardware walletA device that keeps keys
Brute forceTrying every candidate
Rejection samplingDiscarding to stay even

Five of those need a sentence rather than a line. Entropy counts how many equally likely secrets yours was drawn from, which is why it is measured in bits and why it tells you nothing about how the result looks. A checksum catches a slip in copying and nothing else, so a wallet accepting a phrase is never evidence that the phrase is hard to guess. A derivation path selects one branch of the unlimited tree of keys that a single seed contains, which is why the same words can restore into two wallets that show you different addresses. A passphrase is an extra secret mixed into that derivation, and it is unrecoverable once forgotten. And rejection sampling is the habit of discarding outcomes that do not divide evenly, such as a 5 or a 6 on a six-sided die, which is exactly what keeps the outcomes you do keep equally likely.

Two of those carry more weight than the rest. Entropy is what everything else rests on, and the field guide spends a section on what it actually measures, because “looks random” is not it. A passphrase is powerful and unforgiving in equal measure, and is worth reading in full before you decide to use one.
