Some of these get asked before a first phrase exists. Some get asked years later. Each answer is as short as the question allows and no shorter. Where an answer rests on arithmetic, the field guide shows the working. Where it rests on a procedure, start here gives the steps. The second half is a glossary. Most of the confusion around seed phrases is vocabulary, not mathematics.

The short version

  • Word order is part of the secret, so number your backup one to twelve and never sort it.
  • You don't get to choose the words: entropy measures how you picked, not how random the result feels.
  • A passphrase has nothing to check it against, so forgetting one loses the coins for good.
  • One missing word is usually recoverable offline, because you supply the other eleven, and that's recovery, not a shortcut.
  • Printing the word list is safe, but ticking your own words on it is a plaintext copy of your phrase.

Warning

One answer below explains how to recover a phrase that's missing a single word. That works only because you supply the other eleven and know where the gap is. It isn't a way in for anybody else. Nothing on this page shortens the search for a phrase you never knew.

No legitimate party ever needs your recovery phrase. Not a wallet maker, not an exchange, not a support agent, not a “validator” or a “recovery service”. This page has no field that accepts a phrase. It makes no network requests and stores nothing.

01 Questions

Roughly in the order they arrive. Where an answer depends on arithmetic, the field guide has the working. Where it depends on a procedure, start here has the steps.

  • Twelve words or twenty-four? Twelve is fine, and twenty-four is not paranoid. Twelve carry 128 bits and twenty-four carry 256, and the field guide shows why 128 is already past brute force. Twenty-four hedges against future cryptanalysis and Grover-style quantum search, at the cost of more to record and keep legible. Without a reason to prefer one, write twelve.
  • Does the order of the words matter? Completely, because the phrase is one number cut into eleven-bit groups and each word is the group at a fixed position, so moving two words changes the number as surely as changing one. A swap usually fails the checksum, which is a mercy rather than a safeguard. Number your backup as you write it, and never sort it.
  • Can I choose the words myself? No, and it is the most expensive mistake on this page. Entropy is measured against what an attacker knows about how you chose, not against how arbitrary the result feels from the inside. Twelve words picked from the thirty-two you find most memorable is sixty bits, which a serious hardware budget works through, and the result looks exactly like a good phrase.
  • What is a passphrase, the “twenty-fifth word”? An extra secret mixed into the same derivation, so the same twelve words with a different passphrase produce a completely different wallet. What it buys is that the paper alone is no longer enough to spend. What it costs is that nobody can recover it: forget it and the coins are gone. Protect works through both halves.
  • I have lost one word. Am I finished? Probably not, if you know which position is missing and the other eleven are right. There are only 2048 candidates, and the checksum leaves roughly 128 for a twelve-word phrase, so derive each and see which returns addresses you recognise. It works only because you already supply almost the whole secret, and somebody starting from nothing still faces all 2^128.
  • Is it safe to print or hang the whole word list? Yes, and the poster is the public BIP-39 list, so hanging it reveals no more than hanging up a dictionary, and the blank worksheet is equally safe. What is dangerous is a marked list, because twelve ticks on a grid of 2048 is your phrase in plaintext.
  • Can I keep the phrase in a password manager, a photo, or a cloud note? No, and all three are the same mistake wearing different clothes. Each turns a secret that lived in one physical place into a file on a server built to be reachable from anywhere. Write it by hand and keep it offline.
  • Will the same words work in a different wallet? Yes, and that portability is why the standard exists. What people hit is a restore that comes back empty, and the usual cause is the derivation path, since one seed holds an unlimited tree of keys. A valid phrase that isn't yours also restores a real, empty wallet, so an empty wallet is a question, not an answer.
  • Should I split the phrase into halves? Not in a scheme you invented. Six known words of a twelve-word phrase leave about sixty-two bits, which a large hardware budget can reach, and home-made splits lose coins far more often than they prevent theft. The established answers are multisignature and standardised share schemes, both in Protect.
  • How do I know this page is what it claims? Two checks, proving different amounts. The hash is the weaker one: every deploy publishes the file's SHA-256 as index.html.sha256, which says nothing about the server's honesty. The stronger one is cloning the repository and running scripts/verify.sh, which rebuilds the page from source and diffs it. A published hash is a claim. A reproducible build is a check.
  • Does quantum computing change any of this? Not today, and for the phrase the relevant result is Grover-style search, which in theory halves a symmetric secret's effective strength and is the one real argument for twenty-four words. The larger question isn't about your phrase: public-key signatures are the part a large quantum computer would threaten, and that is a protocol problem the whole network would meet together.

One question isn't on this list, and it's asked too rarely: who else has seen this phrase? Treat any phrase you didn't watch being created as already compromised, which covers a friend, a setup service, a tutorial and a card in the box. Roll your own exists so you can watch every bit of one arrive.
