Protection · layers and their costs

Every layer
has a price

Suppose the hard part is done. The phrase was generated properly, it is written on paper, and nobody has seen it. This page is about everything after that: the things people add on top, what each one actually buys, and what each one costs. There are only a few real layers, and every one of them defends against something while creating a new way to lose access. None of this is a recommendation to add all of them. The honest default is to add none of them and get the paper right first.

Warning

Every layer you add is another thing that must survive, be remembered, and still work in ten years. Protection that has never been rehearsed is not protection, it is an intention. If you add a passphrase or a second key and never restore from them, you have not made your coins safer. You have added a way to lose them that did not exist before.

Nothing on this page needs your phrase and nothing here asks for it. As everywhere else on this site, no legitimate party ever needs your recovery phrase, and no field here accepts one.

01 What you are defending against

“Keeping it safe” is not one problem. At least five distinct things can separate you from your coins, they barely overlap, and a measure that answers one of them often does nothing at all for the others. Deciding which one you actually mean is most of the work, and it is the step that gets skipped in favour of buying something.

  • Theft by someone who finds the backup. Not a cryptographer. A burglar working through the obvious drawers, a builder, a lodger, a houseguest, a relative, a removal firm, a landlord with a key. Your paper is the entire secret and it is written in plain language, so anyone who reads it can spend from anywhere in the world, immediately and irreversibly. This is the threat that a passphrase and multisig are designed for, and it is the only one they are both good at.
  • Loss, with no attacker involved. Fire, flood, a burst pipe, a house move, a clear-out, a word written so badly that six years later you cannot tell an l from a 1, a hiding place so clever you cannot reconstruct it, a device that dies with the only copy of something you assumed was written down. Nobody is against you here. The coins go anyway.
  • Coercion. Someone who knows you hold coins and is standing in front of you. Cryptography has no answer to this, and no arrangement makes it pleasant. The only thing that helps is a structure in which it is genuinely true that you cannot produce everything on the spot, and that structure has to be real, because a bluff has to be maintained under conditions where people are bad at maintaining bluffs. The best defence remains not being known to hold anything.
  • A compromised device at signing time. Here the phrase never leaks and the backup is irrelevant. Malware changes the destination address between the moment you copy it and the moment you approve it, or presents a transaction that does something other than what the screen on your computer says. The defence is not a better backup, it is verifying the amount and the full destination address on the signing device's own screen, every time, and refusing to approve anything you cannot read there.
  • Your own death or incapacity. From your family's point of view this is indistinguishable from loss, and the coins are just as gone. It is the threat that receives the least attention per unit of money it actually consumes.

Those five want different answers, and two of them want opposite answers. Anything that makes the secret harder for a stranger to use makes it harder for you to use as well, and harder still for whoever has to use it without you. That tension runs through the rest of this page, and there is no arrangement that escapes it.

Keep the proportions in mind. Coins are lost to fire, water, forgetting, transcription errors, dead devices, clever schemes nobody else understood and quiet deaths far more often than they are lost to burglars, and the questions page says the same thing from another angle. A design that defends only against a thief is unbalanced against the way people actually lose money, and adding layers usually makes that imbalance worse rather than better.

02 The passphrase

Wallets sometimes call it the “25th word”, which is a friendly name for something that is not a word and is not part of the phrase. It is an extra secret you supply alongside the twelve words, mixed into the same derivation that turns them into a seed. The field guide describes that derivation: the words are stretched with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512, and the passphrase goes into the salt. So the same twelve words with a different passphrase produce a completely different seed, and therefore a completely different wallet, with different addresses and no relationship to the first one.

It is taken literally. Case, spacing, punctuation and accents all count, a trailing space is part of the secret, and there is no list of valid passphrases because every possible string is one.

What it buys. Exactly one thing, and it is a real thing: your paper backup on its own is no longer enough to spend. Somebody who finds it, and knows what it is, and tries it, gets a valid and empty wallet. That answers the first threat above, theft by whoever finds the backup, and it answers it well. It also means the twelve words can be stored somewhere less dramatic than you would otherwise accept, which is genuinely useful.

What it does not buy. It does nothing for fire or flood. It does nothing at signing time, when the device in front of you is the problem. It does nothing for your heirs except make their job harder. And against coercion it converts “hand over the paper” into “tell me the passphrase”, which is not obviously an improvement.

What it costs. This is the part that is usually undersold, so it is stated bluntly.

  • There is no checksum on it, and no error to see. The twelve words have four bits of checksum, so a mistyped word is usually caught. A passphrase has nothing of the kind. A wrong one is not rejected, because there is nothing to reject it against. It silently opens a different real wallet, which is empty, and the software will show you that empty wallet with complete confidence. A single missing capital letter and a single stray space are indistinguishable from having lost everything.
  • It is not recoverable, by anyone. No wallet, device, vendor or service records what you chose, because nothing anywhere ever sees it in a form it could store. There is no reset, no support desk, no escrow, no clever forensic path. If it exists only in your memory, then it is a secret with no backup, and memory is not a storage medium: it decays, and it decays fastest for things you rarely use.
  • Forgetting it loses the coins as completely as losing the phrase. Not partially, not with more effort. The twelve words and the passphrase are two halves of one secret, and holding one half is worth nothing. You can be looking directly at a perfect, legible, fireproof backup of your seed phrase and be permanently locked out.
  • It doubles the backup problem. You now have two secrets to record, protect and keep legible for decades, and they must be stored apart or the whole point is lost. Two locations is more than twice the administration of one, because you must also remember that the second one exists and what it belongs to.
  • It has to be typed, correctly, on unfamiliar hardware. Sometimes years later, sometimes on a small device with an awkward keyboard, sometimes by someone who is not you. A passphrase full of unusual characters is a passphrase you may not be able to enter on the device you end up holding.

Plausible deniability. The idea is appealing: the words without the passphrase open a real wallet, so you can keep a small amount there and hand that over under duress, while the serious wallet stays invisible. It works better in explanation than in practice, and it is worth being honest about why. Anyone who knows enough to demand your seed phrase probably knows that passphrases exist, since the feature is documented by every vendor that offers it and discussed everywhere. The decoy has to hold enough to be believed and look like it has been used, which means maintaining it. Nothing about the arrangement proves a negative, so the other person's decision to stop is not something you control. Treat it as a thin extra option rather than the reason to adopt a passphrase.

If you use one, do these four things. Back it up with the same seriousness as the phrase, in writing, exactly as typed, with any leading or trailing spaces made explicit or avoided entirely. Store it apart from the words, so that finding either one alone achieves nothing. Choose something you can transcribe unambiguously and type on any device, which argues for length over exotic characters. And restore from both, together, before the wallet holds anything.

A passphrase converts a theft risk into a loss risk. That is a good trade only if theft of the physical backup is genuinely the threat that worries you most, and only if you are the sort of person who will test it and keep testing it. If you are not sure which of those is true, the honest answer is that far more coins are lost to forgotten passphrases than are saved from burglars by them.

03 Multisig, 2 of 3

A multisignature wallet requires more than one key to spend. The common arrangement, and the one worth understanding first, is 2 of 3: three keys exist, each with its own seed phrase, and any two of them together can sign. One key on its own can do nothing. The three usually live on three separate devices, ideally not all the same make, kept in three separate places.

What it buys. There is no longer a single object whose loss or theft is decisive. A backup found in a drawer is not enough to spend. A device that is stolen, seized, bricked or quietly compromised is not enough to spend, and is also not enough to lose: you sign with the other two, move everything to a fresh set of keys, and the compromised one becomes irrelevant. That is the structural difference from a passphrase. A passphrase adds a second thing you must not lose. Multisig adds a spare.

It is also the only arrangement here that meaningfully addresses two threats at once. Because the keys are in different places, one fire or one flood does not take them all, and because two are needed at the same time, being made to produce one is not the end. That second property is real, and it is also a reason to be careful about who is told the shape of the arrangement.

What it costs. More than people expect, and in an unfamiliar currency: the expensive part is not the money, it is the record-keeping.

  • The seeds alone will not rebuild the wallet. This is the single most important thing on this page about multisig, and the most commonly missed. To reconstruct the wallet you also need to know how the three keys were combined: the policy, the script type, the derivation paths, and the extended public keys of all three cosigners. That information is what a wallet descriptor records. Two seed phrases and no descriptor can be a permanent, total loss with nothing stolen and nothing forgotten. So the descriptor is backed up too, with every seed, in every location.
  • The descriptor is not secret, but it is not harmless either. It cannot spend anything on its own, which is why it can be copied freely among your own backups. It does reveal every address you have ever used and therefore your entire balance and history to anyone who reads it. Store it with care, and never treat “it cannot spend” as “it does not matter”.
  • Recovery is a project rather than a step. Restoring a single-signature wallet is typing twelve words into almost any wallet made in the last decade. Restoring this one means finding software that supports the policy you chose, rebuilding the wallet from the descriptor, importing two of the keys, and producing a transaction that collects two signatures, often by moving a partly signed transaction between devices. Support for this is narrower than for ordinary wallets, and the transactions themselves are larger, so they cost more in fees.
  • Complexity is itself a risk. A scheme that only you fully understand fails on precisely the day you are not there to explain it. Every additional device is another firmware to keep current, another set of instructions, another thing that must still be workable in ten years by someone who may not be you.

Who it suits. Someone holding an amount where an evening of setup and a rehearsed recovery is clearly proportionate. Anyone whose real worry is a single point of failure rather than a single burglar. Custody shared between people, such as a couple, a family, a business, or an arrangement where a third key sits with someone trusted for use only in an emergency. In those cases it is the right tool and nothing else does the job.

Who it does not suit. A first wallet. A small amount, where the added ways to fail plainly outweigh the theft being prevented. Anyone who wants one piece of paper and no ongoing obligation. And anyone who will not rehearse it, because an untested multisig is strictly worse than a tested single seed: more parts, more records, and no evidence any of it works.

There is a common half-measure worth naming: setting up a 2 of 3 wallet, funding it, and never writing down the descriptor or trying a recovery. It feels like the strongest option on this page and is, in practice, the weakest thing described here.

04 Choosing between them

Three options, side by side. The table is the summary, a phrase per cell. It is short in both directions on purpose: the simplest row is not the weakest one, and the most elaborate row is not the safest one.

Three schemes
Scheme Defends against Costs you How it fails Who it suits
Seed alone Remote attack Nothing Found or burnt Most people
Plus passphrase A found copy A second secret Forgotten The careful
2 of 3 multisig Theft and loss Setup, records Lost descriptor Larger sums

A seed on paper is not the placeholder option. Generated properly it is already beyond brute force by margins the field guide works through, so the only realistic ways to lose it are physical and local. Kept in one dry, private place that is not the drawer beside the computer, and copied to a second building, it defeats almost everything on the threat list except a thief who finds it and your own mortality. For most people, most of the time, it is the correct answer and the effort belongs in the backup rather than in a scheme.

A passphrase is the layer that is most often overrated. It is cheap to enable, which is exactly why it is dangerous: the cost arrives years later and all at once. It is a good answer to one threat and it makes the more common threat worse.

Multisig is the layer most often underrated and most often abandoned halfway. It is the only option that removes the single point of failure rather than adding a second one, and it is also the only option that can fail because of a missing file that was never secret in the first place.

The rows are not a ladder and using two of them is not twice as safe. A passphrase on top of a multisig wallet multiplies the record-keeping rather than the security, and it is the combination most likely to be recoverable only by the person who set it up. Add a layer because it answers a threat you have actually named, not because it is the next one along.

05 The backup itself

Before any of the layers above, the object. Almost everything that goes wrong here is mundane, and the two failures that actually happen to ordinary households are heat and water. A house fire and a burst pipe are not exotic threats, they are the standard ones, and they are the reason metal exists as an option at all.

Paper or steel. Handwritten paper is a perfectly good backup and costs nothing, and it is destroyed by fire and ruined by water. Stamped or punched metal survives both, at the cost of an hour and a modest piece of equipment. The reasonable rule is proportion: paper while you are learning and the balance is small, metal once losing it would genuinely hurt. If you use paper, use ordinary pen and paper rather than a receipt printer's thermal paper, which fades to blank on its own.

Write it for a stranger. Number the words 1 to 12 as you write them, in order, spelled exactly as the wallet showed them. Write legibly enough that someone who has never seen the words could read them, because in the worst case that is precisely who will. Check each word against the poster before you commit it to metal, since a word that is not on the list is a transcription error you can still fix at that point and cannot fix afterwards. It is worth recording the wallet type and the derivation path alongside, and the date, since those are what turn a restore that comes back empty into a restore that comes back. Never record the balance, and never record where the other copies are.

Photographs, cloud notes and password managers are not backups of this kind. Each one converts a secret that lived in a single physical place into a file on a machine designed to be reachable from anywhere, which is the exact property you were trying to avoid, and a photograph taken in airplane mode uploads itself the moment the connection returns. The questions page works through all three properly.

Geographic separation. Fire, flood and burglary are local events, so a second copy in a different building removes a whole category of total loss at a stroke. It also doubles the number of objects that can be found, which means the second copy needs the same protection as the first and not less. A copy at a relative's house is a copy in a house whose locks, lodgers, clear-outs and future occupants you do not control. Two copies you have thought about carefully beat four you have not.

Splitting the phrase yourself is not a backup strategy. Half in one place and half in another feels prudent and is, on both counts, worse than it looks: the halves are weaker than people assume, and home-made splits lose coins far more often than they prevent theft, because the scheme lives in a single head. The questions page gives the arithmetic. If the goal is genuinely to divide custody, that is what multisig above is for.

One more copy of the words exists that people forget about: whatever you wrote the entropy on. If you generated the phrase by hand with the roller or a printed worksheet, that sheet of bits is a second copy of your seed in longhand. It gets destroyed once the phrase is recorded and verified, and never before.

06 Test the recovery

This is the step almost everybody skips, and it is the one that decides whether any of the rest was real. Everything above is a claim about the future. A restore is the only evidence you will ever have.

Restore before you fund it. While the wallet is empty the test costs nothing and a failure is a lucky discovery. After it is funded the test has stakes, so it quietly never happens, and the first real restore attempt takes place on the day something has already gone wrong.

Restore from the backup you actually wrote. Not from the screen, not from memory, not from the copy on the device that is still working. The thing being tested is the object you will one day be holding, including your handwriting, your abbreviations, whether you noted the derivation path, and whether the passphrase you wrote down is the passphrase you typed. Wipe the device and restore, or restore onto a second device, or into a clean offline installation. A restore that reads from something you will not have later has tested nothing.

What success looks like. The same wallet comes back: the same addresses in the same order, the same first receive address, and for a wallet that has been used, the same history. If you can, go one step further and send a small amount out afterwards, because receiving only proves you copied an address correctly and spending is what proves you hold the key. For a multisig wallet the test is the whole rebuild, from the descriptor and two seeds on a machine that has never seen this wallet before, ending in a signature from each of two devices.

An empty wallet after a restore is usually not the phrase. Two causes account for nearly all of it. The derivation path or address type differs, so the wallet is showing you a different branch of the same tree and both are correct. Or a passphrase is involved and something about it is off by a character. Check those two before concluding anything, and remember that a valid phrase which is not yours also restores a real, empty wallet without complaint, so an empty result is a question rather than an answer. The questions page covers the derivation path case in full.

Repeat it occasionally. Once is proof about one day. Do it again after a house move, after you change devices, after firmware changes something about how a device behaves, and after any change to who would need to do this without you. An annual habit is more than most people manage and far more than most people need.

Test the layers specifically, because they are the parts that fail silently. A wrong word is usually caught by the checksum. A wrong passphrase, a missing descriptor and a cosigner key nobody can find are caught by nothing at all, right up until the moment they matter. Start here puts the same test in the middle of both of its recipes, for the same reason.

07 Inheritance and incapacity

A backup that nobody else can find, or can find but cannot use, is indistinguishable from a lost one. The coins are gone in exactly the same way, and the people affected will not even know what they are looking for.

Whatever you leave has to be written for someone who does not know what any of this is. Not a shorter version of what you would write for yourself: an explanation that starts from the beginning. What the words are and what they control. That they must never be typed into a website or sent to anyone offering to help, because that is the moment this most often ends badly. Which software to use and which derivation path. Whether a passphrase exists at all, and where. For multisig, where each key lives, where the descriptor is, and that two of the three are needed. Then have that person do a dry run on an empty wallet while you are still there to watch, because instructions are subject to the same rule as everything else on this page: untested means unknown.

The tension is unavoidable and worth stating plainly. Every instruction that makes recovery possible for your family also makes theft easier for whoever else reads it, and a letter explaining exactly where the secrets are kept is itself a target. The usual shape of the answer is to separate the two: instructions that are findable and explain what to do, secrets that are not stored with them. What arrangement is appropriate, and how any of it interacts with the law where you live, is outside what this page can tell you.

This section is short because it is mostly not a technical problem. It is still the one worth acting on soonest, since it is the only threat on this page whose arrival you are guaranteed not to be able to respond to.
