Protection · layers and their costs

Every layer
has a price

Suppose the hard part is done. The phrase was generated properly, it's written on paper, and nobody has seen it. This page is about what people add on top: what each layer buys, and what each one costs. There are only a few real layers. Every one of them blocks a threat by creating a new way to lose access. That's the trade, every time. None of this is a recommendation to add all of them. The honest default is to add none of them and get the paper right first.

The short version

  • Name the threat before you add a layer. A passphrase answers a found backup and nothing else. Multisig answers a found backup and a lost key. Neither one answers a hacked computer or your own death.
  • A passphrase buys you a paper backup that can't spend on its own, and it costs you a second secret with no checksum, no reset and no way back if you forget it.
  • 2 of 3 multisig buys you a spare key instead of a second thing to lose. It costs you an evening of setup, three locations and records you keep for as long as you hold the coins.
  • The descriptor is part of the backup, because two seed phrases without it can be a total loss with nothing stolen and nothing forgotten.
  • An untested layer is a way to lose coins, so restore from the backup you wrote before the wallet holds anything.
  • Adding nothing is a real option, and for most people it's the right one: one good copy of the paper in a dry private place, and a second copy in another building.

Warning

Every layer you add is one more thing that has to survive, be remembered, and still work in ten years. Protection you've never rehearsed isn't protection. It's an intention. Add a passphrase or a second key, never restore from them, and you haven't made your coins safer. You've added a way to lose them that didn't exist before.

Nothing on this page needs your phrase and nothing here asks for it. As everywhere else on this site: no legitimate party ever needs your recovery phrase, and no field here accepts one.

01 What you are defending against

“Keeping it safe” isn't one problem. Five different things can separate you from your coins, they barely overlap, and a measure that answers one often does nothing for the rest. Deciding which one you mean is most of the work, and it's the step that gets skipped in favour of buying something.

  • Theft by someone who finds the backup. Not a cryptographer: a burglar working the obvious drawers, a builder, a lodger, a relative, a landlord with a key. Your paper is the entire secret, written in plain language, and anyone who reads it can spend from anywhere at once. This is the threat a passphrase and multisig are built for, and the only one they are both good at.
  • Loss, with no attacker involved. Fire, flood, a house move, a clear-out, a word written so badly that six years later you can't tell an l from a 1, a hiding place too clever to reconstruct. Every layer you add makes this threat worse rather than better.
  • Coercion. Somebody who knows you hold coins and is willing to stand in front of you. No arrangement of keys makes you unable to comply, and the honest goal is to be unable to comply quickly.
  • A compromised device at signing time. The machine shows you one address and signs for another. Neither a passphrase nor a stronger backup touches this, because the secret was never the weak point.
  • Your own death or incapacity. The coins are gone the same way, and the people affected won't know what they are looking for.

02 The passphrase

Wallets sometimes call it the “25th word”, a friendly name for something that is neither a word nor part of the phrase. It is an extra secret you supply alongside the twelve words, mixed into the same derivation: the words are stretched with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512 and the passphrase goes into the salt. The same twelve words with a different passphrase produce a completely different wallet, with different addresses and no relationship to the first.

It is taken literally, so case, spacing, punctuation and accents all count, and a trailing space is part of the secret. There is no list of valid passphrases, because every possible string is one.

What it buys. One real thing: your paper backup on its own is no longer enough to spend. Somebody who finds it and tries it gets a valid, empty wallet. That answers the first threat well, and it answers no other.

What it costs. The absence of any recovery path, so read this part twice.

  • It isn't recoverable, by anyone. No wallet, device, vendor or service records what you chose, because nothing anywhere ever sees it in a form it could store. No reset, no support desk, no escrow, no clever forensic path. If it exists only in your memory, it is a secret with no backup, and memory decays fastest for the things you rarely use.
  • Forget it and the coins are gone, as completely as if you had burnt the phrase. Not partially, not with more effort, not with the right expert. The twelve words and the passphrase are two halves of one secret. You can be holding a perfect, legible, fireproof backup of your seed phrase, reading it, and be locked out forever.
  • It doubles the backup problem. Two secrets that must both survive, and storing them together undoes the point of having two.
  • A typo is silent. Every string is valid, so a wrong passphrase opens a different empty wallet rather than reporting an error.

If you use one, write it down, store it in a second place away from the words, and restore from both before you fund anything. A passphrase you have not rehearsed is not a layer. It is a second way to lose everything.

03 Multisig, 2 of 3

A multisignature wallet needs more than one key to spend. The arrangement to understand first is 2 of 3: three keys exist, each with its own seed phrase, and any two together can sign while one alone can do nothing. They usually live on three separate devices, ideally not all the same make, kept in three separate places.

What it buys. There is no longer a single object whose loss or theft decides everything. A backup found in a drawer isn't enough to spend, and a device that is stolen, seized, bricked or quietly compromised isn't enough to spend or to lose. You sign with the other two, move everything to a fresh set of keys, and the compromised one stops mattering. A passphrase adds a second thing you must not lose. Multisig adds a spare.

What it costs. Setup and records, both of which are real work.

  • The descriptor is part of the backup. Three seed phrases are not enough to rebuild the wallet. You also need the descriptor, which says which keys, which script type and which derivation paths. Lose it and recovery becomes an archaeology project, so store a copy with every seed.
  • Three backups, three places, one procedure. The work does not divide by three, it multiplies, and every key needs its own tested restore.
  • Wallet software matters more. Coordinating a signature across devices is where the fiddly failures live, and the tooling is less forgiving than a single-key wallet.

Rehearse the recovery from the descriptor and two seeds on a machine that has never seen the wallet, before it holds anything. That rehearsal is the only thing that turns three keys into a safer arrangement rather than three times the paperwork.

04 Choosing between them

Three options, side by side. The simplest row isn't the weakest, and the most elaborate row isn't the safest.

Three schemes
Scheme Defends against Costs you How it fails Who it suits
Seed alone Remote attack Nothing Found or burnt Most people
Plus passphrase A found copy A second secret Forgotten The careful
2 of 3 multisig Theft and loss Setup, records Lost descriptor Larger sums

A seed on paper isn't the placeholder option. Generated properly it's already beyond brute force by margins the field guide works through, so the only realistic ways to lose it are physical and local. Keep it in one dry, private place that isn't the drawer beside the computer, copy it to a second building, and it defeats almost everything on the threat list. Two exceptions: a thief who finds it, and your own mortality. For most people, most of the time, it's the correct answer, and the effort belongs in the backup rather than in a scheme.

A passphrase is the layer most often overrated. It's cheap to enable, which is exactly why it's dangerous. The cost arrives years later and all at once. A good answer to one threat. It makes the more common threat worse.

Multisig is the layer most often underrated, and most often abandoned halfway. It's the only option that removes the single point of failure instead of adding a second one. It's also the only option that can fail because of a missing file that was never secret in the first place.

The rows aren't a ladder, and using two of them isn't twice as safe. A passphrase on top of a multisig wallet multiplies the record-keeping rather than the security, and it's the combination most likely to be recoverable only by the person who set it up. Add a layer because it answers a threat you have named. Not because it's the next one along.

05 The backup itself

Before any of the layers above, the object itself. The two failures that happen to ordinary households are heat and water, and that is the reason metal exists as an option at all.

Paper or steel. Handwritten paper is a perfectly good backup and costs nothing, though fire destroys it and water ruins it. Stamped or punched metal survives both for an hour of work and a modest piece of equipment. The rule is proportion: paper while you are learning, metal once losing it would hurt. Use ordinary pen and paper rather than a receipt printer's thermal paper, which fades to blank on its own.

Write it for a stranger. Number the words 1 to 12 in order, spelled exactly as the wallet showed them. Write legibly enough for someone who has never seen them, because in the worst case that is precisely who will. Check each word against the poster before you trust it to metal, since only 2048 spellings exist and a word that isn't on the list will never restore.

Where it lives. Somewhere dry and private that isn't the drawer beside the computer, and a second copy in a second building if the amount justifies it. Two copies in one house is one fire. Never photograph it, never type it, and never store it in a password manager or a cloud note, because all three turn a local secret into a remote one.

06 Test the recovery

This is the step almost everybody skips, and it decides whether any of the rest was real. Everything above is a claim about the future, and a restore is the only evidence you will ever have.

Restore before you fund it. While the wallet is empty the test costs nothing and a failure is a lucky discovery. After it is funded the test has stakes, so it quietly never happens, and the first real attempt takes place on the day something has already gone wrong.

Restore from the backup you wrote, not from the screen, not from memory, and not from the copy on the device that's still working. What is being tested is the object you will one day be holding: your handwriting, your abbreviations, whether you noted the derivation path, whether the passphrase you wrote down is the one you typed. A restore that reads from something you won't have later has tested nothing.

What success looks like. The same wallet comes back, with the same addresses in the same order and the same history. If you can, send a small amount afterwards, because receiving only proves you copied an address correctly while spending proves you hold the key. For multisig the test is the whole reconstruction, from the descriptor and two seeds, on a machine that has never seen this wallet.

07 Inheritance and incapacity

A backup nobody else can find, or can find but cannot use, is a lost one. Whatever you leave has to be written for someone who doesn't know what any of this is, starting from the beginning. What the words are and what they control. That they must never be typed into a website or sent to anyone offering to help. Which software and which derivation path to use, whether a passphrase exists and where, and for multisig where each key and the descriptor live.

Then have that person do a dry run on an empty wallet while you are still there to watch. Instructions follow the same rule as everything else here: untested means unknown.

The tension is unavoidable and worth stating plainly. Every instruction that makes recovery possible for your family makes theft possible for whoever else reads it, so the instructions and the secret belong in different places. The layer you cannot explain is the layer they will not recover.
